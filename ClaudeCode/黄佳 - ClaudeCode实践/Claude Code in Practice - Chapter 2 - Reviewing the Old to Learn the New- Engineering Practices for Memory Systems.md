
## Reviewing the Old to Learn the New: Engineering Practices for Memory Systems

**Past experience, if not forgotten, is a guide for the future.** 
After the last technical discussion, Xiao Bing couldn't wait to try Claude Code in the order service project she was responsible for. Full of expectation, she entered her first request: "Help me write a user login interface." A few seconds later, Claude generated seemingly perfect code: based on the Express framework, using JavaScript syntax, and accompanied by the `npm install` command, all in one go. Xiao Bing stared at the screen blankly for 3 seconds before slowly typing the next line: "Our project uses Fastify and TypeScript..." She then patiently corrected the model's constraints and redescribed the requirements, finally obtaining the expected code. The next day, Xiao Bing closed the terminal and started a brand new session, asking Claude to write an order creation interface. This time, Claude was even more enthusiastic, generating complete routes, controllers, and data models at length; however, the framework was still Express, the package manager was still npm, and even the directory structure was vastly different from the team's specifications. Staring at the familiar line `const express = require('express')` on the screen, a deep sense of powerlessness welled up in Xiao Bing's heart. This "AI amnesia" does not stem from Claude's lack of capability. On the contrary, it demonstrates excellent programming skills in every independent session; however, every seemingly "reasonable" choice it makes—choosing Express over Fastify, using npm instead of pnpm, and adopting JavaScript instead of TypeScript—invariably hits the "red lines" of team conventions.

#### 2.1 Why AI Needs "Memory"

Full of complaints, Xiao Bing went to Brother Ka. After hearing her experience, Brother Ka was not in a hurry to provide a solution. "Xiao Bing, do you remember how you felt on your first day of work?" asked Brother Ka. Xiao Bing thought for a moment: "Of course I remember, I didn't know anything at that time—where the code repository was, what the branching strategy was, how the CI process ran... I had to ask my colleagues about everything." "Exactly. Now imagine, if you woke up every morning and forgot everything you learned at the company the day before, and every day felt like your 'first day of work'—what would you do?" Xiao Bing couldn't help but shiver: "Isn't that exactly my current state using Claude?" "Absolutely correct," Brother Ka explained. "For Claude, every conversation is its 'first day of work' (see Figure 2-1). This is just like a highly skilled new employee who shows up on the first day knowing nothing about the company's tech stack, having to guess based solely on personal experience and industry conventions. The guesses might be logical, but not necessarily consistent with the actual situation." ![[Pasted image 20260701090454.png]] _(Figure 2-1 Every conversation is Claude's "first day of work")_ This is a fundamental characteristic of large models: the lack of persistent state between sessions. Whenever you close a Claude Code session and start a new one, the Claude model faces a completely blank canvas. It doesn't remember what framework your project uses, what coding style your team prefers, or even the mistakes you corrected 5 minutes ago. This is not a defect of any specific model, but a commonality among all current large models—every round of dialogue must build context from scratch.

From a software engineering perspective, this is exactly a counter-example of the "Convention over Configuration" principle. The reason Ruby on Rails empowers developers with extremely high efficiency is due to its pre-set suite of reasonable conventions—for example, using plural names for database tables, singular names for models, and placing controllers under the `app/controllers` directory; developers only need to focus on the special parts that deviate from the conventions. However, Claude's default "conventions" do not originate from project specifications, but from its training data, which represents the statistical patterns of publicly available code across the entire internet. In this statistical distribution, the occurrence frequency of Express is higher than Fastify, npm is more common than pnpm, and the sample size of JavaScript far exceeds that of TypeScript. Therefore, lacking specific project context, Claude will naturally lean toward selecting the most "mainstream" solutions. These solutions might be logical in general scenarios, but may not fit your team's specific standards. In other words, what Claude lacks is not capability, but context. That being the case, the solution naturally follows: since every conversation is Claude's "first day of work," we might as well prepare an exclusive "onboarding manual" for it, ensuring it automatically reads it every time it starts. This manual is `CLAUDE.md`. As a plain Markdown formatted text file, you simply need to place it in the project's designated directory; Claude Code will automatically scan and load its contents upon startup, injecting it into the very front of the dialogue as part of the system context. From then on, Claude no longer needs to guess what the project looks like from scratch—all specifications and details are clearly stated in the manual.

This design approach is already quite common in software engineering. We use `.editorconfig` to unify editor behavior, leverage `.eslintrc` to standardize coding styles, constrain TypeScript compilation options via `tsconfig.json`, and rely on `.prettierrc` to solidify formatting rules. The essence of these configuration files is to externalize implicit "team conventions" from human brains into machine-readable declarative rules. `CLAUDE.md` follows the same logic; the only difference lies in its "reader": it is no longer an editor or a formatting tool, but a large model equipped with natural language understanding capabilities. This is exactly the natural extension and evolution of the "Configuration-Driven" mindset in the AI era. **Brother Ka's Remarks** Some people might ask: "Doesn't Claude Code have the ability to read project files? It can perfectly well consult `package.json` to infer the tech stack on its own." That is true, but "active probing" not only consumes valuable tool call limits and context windows; more importantly, `package.json` only records dependency lists and cannot carry deeper information such as architectural decisions and team conventions. The core value of `CLAUDE.md` lies precisely in coalescing implicit knowledge scattered everywhere into an explicit guiding document, ensuring Claude can build an accurate mental model of the project before it starts coding.

#### 2.2 Five-Layer Memory System

"I understand the principle now, so where exactly should `CLAUDE.md` be placed?" Xiao Bing followed up.

Brother Ka smiled slightly: "Good question. The answer is—it doesn't exist in just one place." Claude Code's memory system adopts an ingenious layered architecture, divided into 5 levels from global to local. This design shares similarities with the configuration loading mechanism of an operating system: just as a Linux operating system, when reading configurations, will sequentially scan global configurations under the `/etc/` directory, personal configurations under the user's home directory, and project configurations under the current directory, achieving gradual refinement through layered stacking; Claude Code's five-layer memory system follows the exact same design philosophy. We can use a consistent analogy to explain this system: the hierarchy of a company's institutional documents. The first layer is the "Group Code of Conduct" issued by the group headquarters. This is an ironclad rule that all employees must obey; no branch office may modify it without authorization. It establishes the enterprise's core values and bottom line. The second layer is the "Department Operational Manual" of each department. Its validity covers all projects within the department, ensuring standardized uniformity under the same business line. The third layer is the "Project Development Specifications" of the project team. It refines rules down to a single product, clarifying core conventions such as tech stack selection and coding styles. The fourth layer is the "Special Operation Guidelines" targeting specific scenarios. For instance, the "Database Migration Operational Specifications" is activated and consulted only when specific tasks are involved, possessing strong scenario specificity. The fifth layer consists of personal memo notes on each employee's desk. This is completely personalized knowledge accumulation, visible only to oneself, used to record temporary debugging tips or personal preferences.

From top to bottom, the applicable scope gradually narrows, the granularity of content becomes increasingly fine, and management flexibility increases accordingly. Figure 2-2 illustrates the five-layer memory system of Claude Code. ![[Pasted image 20260701090615.png]] _(Figure 2-2 Claude Code's five-layer memory system)_ Next, we will analyze the responsibilities and typical usages of each layer one by one. **Enterprise Level** constitutes the "ceiling" of the entire system, and is also a layer that developers usually cannot access. This layer is uniformly set by the company's IT administrators via system-level configuration files (typically `/etc/claude-code/CLAUDE.md` in a Linux OS) or through the Anthropic enterprise management backend. Typical policies include: prohibiting the transmission of code to external APIs, requiring all database operations to be executed via an ORM, strictly forbidding hardcoding secrets in code, etc. The existence of this level establishes the enterprise's compliance baseline: no matter how developers configure the lower 4 layers, they cannot bypass the mandatory constraints here. For individual developers and small teams, this layer is usually left blank and requires no special attention. **User Level** is equivalent to the user's "personal preference profile." This configuration file is located at `~/.claude/CLAUDE.md` under the user's home directory and takes effect for all projects opened on this machine. This place is best suited for storing general preferences that are independent of specific business logic but reflect personal working habits, such as the choice of communication language (Chinese or English), format specifications for Git commit messages, or mapping personal favorite shortcut commands to specific execution commands. These preferences possess high stability and will not change when switching projects.

**Project Level** is the most frequently used in daily development, and it is the main protagonist of this chapter. It manifests as `CLAUDE.md` in the root directory of the project, which must be committed to the Git repository to become part of the project's code assets. This means that when new colleagues clone the project, Claude Code will automatically read this file; without any additional configuration, Claude instantly gains complete project context. It's like a new employee receiving a dedicated "Project Development Manual" in their desk welcome package on their first day—flipping it open, tech stack selections, directory structures, coding standards, and common commands are immediately clear at a glance. We will elaborate on how to meticulously write this crucial file in Section 2.3. **Rule Level** is an ingenious advanced design aimed at solving the pain point where "a single `CLAUDE.md` struggles to carry all complex rules." You can deploy multiple independent Markdown files under the `.claude/rules/` directory, letting each file focus on a specific topic (like database specifications, API design styles, or testing strategies). More crucially, this layer supports conditional trigger mechanisms—rule files are only dynamically loaded when operating on specific files or entering specific contexts, thereby avoiding the redundant occupation of the context window by irrelevant information. We will specifically explore an in-depth analysis and practical usage of this mechanism in Section 2.4. **Local Level** is the developer's "private memo," corresponding to `CLAUDE.local.md`. This file will not be committed to the Git repository (Claude Code automatically includes it in `.gitignore` rules). It is specifically designed for information that is highly valuable to individuals but not suitable for sharing, such as test server addresses and ports, exclusive insights accumulated during personal debugging processes, or temporary work memos.

For example, if you know the development environment's database will undergo maintenance during a specific period, such information only needs to be recorded in the local layer; there is no need to inform the whole team, thus avoiding information noise. Hearing this, Xiao Bing sharply raised a key question: "What should be done if conflicts occur among the contents of these 5 layers? For instance, I set '4 spaces indentation' at the user level, while the project level mandates '2 spaces indentation'." "Good question," Brother Ka approved. "These files follow the principle of 'stacking rather than overriding.' Claude Code reads the contents of all layers and merges them into a complete context. When contradictions arise, the Claude model will make decisions based on its judgment, and its core logic is specificity-first. In the indentation example, the project-level '2 spaces' rule targets the current specific project, making its specificity higher than the user-level's general '4 spaces' preference; therefore, Claude will prioritize the former. However, the enterprise level is an exception. It possesses absolute coercive power; no matter how lower layers are configured, they cannot bypass its constraints. This is akin to a company's legal red line or security baseline, which no project manager or personal preference has the right to transgress." It is worth mentioning that `CLAUDE.md` also has a powerful built-in **file reference mechanism**. In memory files at any level, you can use the `@path/to/file` syntax to import the contents of other files, thereby achieving a modular organization of configurations. For example, writing `@docs/api-conventions.md` in the project-level `CLAUDE.md` will cause Claude to automatically load and parse the contents of the API conventions document. This mechanism supports up to five layers of nested recursion, which is sufficient to handle the organizational needs of the vast majority of complex projects.

This provides an elegant document governance solution for large-scale projects: `CLAUDE.md` no longer needs to carry all details, but serves as an "index master outline," flexibly pointing to various thematic documents. This design successfully avoids the drawback of piling massive amounts of information into a single file, making the configuration structure clearer and more maintainable.

#### 2.3 `CLAUDE.md` Writing Paradigm

Having understood the storage locations and layer responsibilities of memory files, a more core proposition surfaces: how to write them? Brother Ka solemnly wrote down four big characters on the whiteboard: **Less is More**. "The contents of `CLAUDE.md` possess global persistence," explained Brother Ka. "They will be completely injected into the context window at the initialization of every conversation. This means that every line of text you write will consume precious Token quotas in every interaction." He made a vivid analogy: "You can imagine the context window as an office desk with limited space. The surface area of the desk is fixed. If you pile thick reference materials (i.e., lengthy rule files) on the desk before Claude Code even starts its actual work, there will be very little space left for the code, files, and temporary drafts that actually need to be processed. Once the desk is fully occupied, Claude Code has to 'throw away' some early key information to free up space, which often leads to it 'forgetting' important instructions or context details." This analogy is not only vivid but also backed by solid data. According to Anthropic's engineering practice data, Claude Code's System Prompt has already built-in approximately 50 core instructions—these constitute the "OS-level" cornerstone for the model's normal operation.

When user-customized `CLAUDE.md` contents are added, if the total number of instructions breaches the critical threshold of 150, the model's adherence quality to instructions will exhibit significant degradation. The logic behind this is: instruction density is inversely proportional to adherence probability. The more verbose and convoluted the instructions are, the lower the probability that each rule will be "deeply focused on" and "strictly executed" by the model. This perfectly matches the "Information Overload" principle in management theory: when an employee handbook is so thick that no one can read it through, its actual execution effect is often worse than an A4-sized summary that extracts the core essence. In AI collaboration, conciseness is not only a means to save Tokens, but also a key strategy to ensure instructions are effectively implemented. So, what contents are exactly worth writing into `CLAUDE.md`? Brother Ka provided an efficient "Three Questions Framework" as a filtering criterion. **Question 1: WHY (Why do it this way)** Core Value: Endow the Claude model with the reasoning capability to "draw inferences from one instance." This kind of information aims to reveal the underlying logic behind decisions, helping Claude understand the core concerns of the project. Once it grasps the "why," even when encountering unspecified edge cases, the model can still deduce correctly based on principles. Example: "We choose Fastify instead of Express because Fastify's schema-based validation aligns with this project's 'type safety first' strategy." Effect: When Claude understands that "type safety" is the highest priority, even if you don't specify a library somewhere, it will proactively lean toward choosing solutions with better type definitions, rather than just mechanically matching keywords.

**Question 2: WHAT (What to do, what not to do)** Core Value: Delineate insurmountable "behavioral boundaries." These are the most direct and rigid behavioral instructions, clearly defining the scope of what is permitted and what is prohibited. Such rules typically do not require an explanation of the reason; they demand absolute obedience and execution. The vast majority of project specifications fall into this category. Example: "You must use pnpm as the package manager; using npm or yarn is prohibited." Effect: Establish clear "red lines," eliminate the model's uncertainty in tool selection, and ensure the unity of the tech stack. **Question 3: HOW (How to do it step by step)** Core Value: Solidify "Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs)." This type of content is applicable to common commands, fixed processes, or specific operational paradigms. The model does not need to understand the complex principles behind the commands; it only needs to accurately execute the established steps. Example: "To execute database migration, please run `pnpm db:migrate`; to populate test data, please run `pnpm db:seed`." Effect: Transform the team's best practices into the model's "muscle memory," reducing low-level mistakes caused by command spelling errors or missing parameters, and improving execution efficiency. Not all information requires answering these 3 questions simultaneously. Most rules only need to be clearly defined with a single sentence at the WHAT level. Only those key decisions that are easily misunderstood or where the model might "do something bad with good intentions" are worth supplementing with deep explanations at the WHY level. If you find that in your own `CLAUDE.md`, every rule is accompanied by a large paragraph of background explanation or emotional expression, it usually means the content is overloaded. Conciseness is the royal road to efficient collaboration.

Below, we will help you quickly build intuition by comparing a negative example with a subsequent positive example. Let's first look at a negative example: vague "correct nonsense." This `CLAUDE.md` is filled with pleasantries and vague instructions found in human social contexts, which for AI, is almost equivalent to invalid information.

- "Write it better": Better to what extent? Does it mean conforming to Prettier formatting, or applying design patterns?
- "Comprehensive": What scenarios should be covered? Is it unit testing, integration testing, or E2E testing? Is the coverage requirement 80% or 100%?
- "Best practices": Whose best practices? Claude after reading this passage, gains almost zero actionable information. What is especially fatal is the instruction "follow best practices." It seems correct, but it is actually hollow. Because Claude will by default try to follow the "best practices" it recognizes (based on statistical patterns trained on massive internet data). The core of the problem lies exactly here: there is often a deviation between the model's general "best practices" and your team's specific "technical conventions." Furthermore, metadata like "the team has 5 people" or "development started in March 2024" provides no guiding significance for the code generation logic, yet it vainly occupies precious context space. Now let's look at a positive example: a precise "operational manual." The gap between the two files is clear at a glance. The excellent version is concise, specific, and highly operable; every rule directly anchors the behavior logic of the Claude model.
- Tech stack locking: Upon seeing "Fastify 4 framework (do not use Express)," Claude will immediately eliminate Express-related solutions and directly generate Fastify-based code.
- Implementation of architectural standards: After seeing the directory structure description, Claude can accurately determine that new route files should be placed under the `src/routes/` directory, rather than being haphazardly stacked.
- Zero-error execution: After seeing common commands and their comments, Claude can accurately execute build and test tasks, putting an end to the possibility of blindly guessing between `npm run build` and `yarn build`.

In addition to manual writing, Claude Code also provides two convenient tools to assist in managing memory files. The first is the `/init` command. After executing this command in the project root directory, Claude Code will automatically scan the project's directory structure, dependency configuration files (such as `package.json`, `pyproject.toml`, `Cargo.toml`, etc.), and other key configurations, immediately generating a first draft of `CLAUDE.md`. This first draft typically covers tech stack identification, directory structure overview, and common script commands, serving as an excellent starting foundation. However, it still requires manual review and supplementation of contents that `/init` cannot automatically infer, such as specific constraints of layered architecture, unified conventions for API response formats, team-specific naming conventions, etc. These "implicit knowledge" only exist in the minds of team members and cannot be extracted purely from configuration files. They must be made explicit through human intervention to ensure that the AI's behavior fully matches the team's expectations. The second is the `/memory` command, used to dynamically update memory during a conversation. After executing this command, Claude Code will pop up a file selector, listing all currently available memory files for you to choose to edit. These files cover different scopes of application, as specifically shown in Table 2-1. **Table 2-1 Scope of Application of Project Memory**

|File Type|Path|Scope of Application|
|---|---|---|
|Project Memory|`./CLAUDE.md` or `./.claude/CLAUDE.md`|Team Shared: Can be included in version control, suitable for unifying team standards|
|User Memory|`~/.claude/CLAUDE.md`|Personal Global: Personal preference configurations, takes effect for all projects of the current user|

After selecting a file, the system will open it directly in the editor for you to modify instantly. Suppose you find in an interaction that Claude Code has again mistakenly used moment.js, while project standards explicitly require using date-fns. At this point, you do not need to exit the conversation or manually search for files; you only need to execute the following steps: Step 1, enter the `/memory` command. Step 2, select the project-level `CLAUDE.md`. Step 3, append a rule: Date processing uniformly uses date-fns; using moment.js is prohibited. Step 4, save and close. The next time a conversation starts, this new rule takes effect immediately. If this preference merely belongs to a personal habit and you don't wish to affect the team, you can choose to write it into `CLAUDE.local.md`—this file is excluded by `.gitignore` by default and will not be committed to the code repository. This mechanism builds a virtuous closed loop of "making a mistake -> correcting -> writing to memory -> avoiding making it again." Over time, your memory files will become increasingly precise, and Claude Code's performance in the project will also continue to evolve accordingly.

#### 2.4 Conditional Rules System

As the project evolves, a single `CLAUDE.md` will eventually face capacity bottlenecks. When a project covers all rules regarding front-end component specifications, back-end API design, database migration processes, testing strategies (coverage/mock), and infrastructure (Docker and CI/CD), piling all the content into a single file will trigger two core problems:

- Maintenance nightmare: The file becomes extremely lengthy, making it difficult to search and update specific rules.
- Attention distraction: For example, when Claude is writing a simple front-end component, its context is filled with irrelevant information like database migration specs and CI configuration requirements. This noise will dilute the model's attention, causing its efficiency to drop when handling the current task, and possibly even generating hallucinations or ignoring crucial front-end constraints.

This is exactly where the core value of the `.claude/rules/` directory lies. It evolves the memory system from a "comprehensive and heavy manual" into an "on-demand modular knowledge base." This mechanism possesses two core advantages. Domain decomposition: Allows a massive rule set to be broken down into multiple thematic files, with each file focusing on a specific domain, greatly improving maintainability. Intelligent activation: By adding YAML Frontmatter to the head of the file, leveraging the `paths` field to declare Glob patterns; only when the file path operated by Claude Code matches this pattern will the corresponding rules be activated and injected into the context; in non-matching scenarios, these rule files are stored quietly on the disk, consuming neither tokens nor context space. Under this configuration, when Claude edits `src/services/order-service.ts`, this test specification will not be loaded; but once it turns to writing `tests/order-service.test.ts`, these rules automatically take effect instantly. This "Lazy Loading" philosophy is perfectly aligned: there is no need to preload all potential resources upon startup; they only need to be dynamically called upon when truly needed. The same mindset can be generalized to other development domains. For example, database specifications are loaded only when operating on Prisma files or the data access layer, while API design specifications only take effect when editing route or Schema files.

After hearing this, Xiao Xue summarized incisively: "This is like tailor-making exclusive operational manuals for employees in different positions within an enterprise—the front desk follows front desk processes, the warehouse strictly abides by warehouse standards, and not everyone is required to memorize the entire company's regulations. Only when you step into the warehouse to work do you need to flip open the 'Warehouse Management Manual'." Brother Ka nodded and supplemented: "This modular method of organizing rules has another major practical benefit: in multi-person collaboration scenarios, domain experts can independently maintain their exclusive rule files, thereby significantly reducing the probability of Git merge conflicts. Front-end engineers specialize in `frontend.md`, back-end engineers maintain `api-design.md`, and DBAs control `database.md`—everyone performs their own duties without interfering with each other. As the team scales up, the value of this 'divide and conquer' strategy will become increasingly prominent." _Brother Ka's Remarks_ If a rule file is not configured with `paths` frontmatter metadata, the system treats it as a global unconditional rule, forcibly loading it in every session—its effect is equivalent to writing it directly into the main `CLAUDE.md` file. Therefore, you must set precise `paths` limits for every rule file to truly leverage the core advantage of "on-demand loading" in the memory system.

#### Chapter Summary

Returning to the scenario at the beginning of this chapter: every time Xiao Bing started a brand new session, Claude habitually substituted Express for Fastify, and npm for pnpm. Now we understand that this is not a capability defect of the Claude model, but because it lacked a crucial "onboarding manual." `CLAUDE.md` is exactly the key mechanism to solve this pain point. It solidifies the team's tech stack selection, coding standards, project structure, and common commands into a Claude-readable configuration document. Whenever a session starts, Claude Code will automatically load this information, thereby completely skipping those ineffective communications of repeated corrections and entering directly into a highly efficient collaborative state. The design of the five-layer memory system profoundly reflects the classic principle of "Separation of Concerns" in software engineering.

- Enterprise Level: Strictly guards security baselines, managed uniformly by the organization.
- User Level: Accumulates personal preferences, respecting individual differences.
- Project Level: Carries team consensus, ensuring collaborative consistency.
- Rule Level: Realizes the modularization and conditional loading of rules.
- Local Level: Accommodates private memos, providing flexible temporary context. Each level owns clear responsibility boundaries and management subjects, powerfully supporting team collaboration while fully respecting personal habits. Among them, the conditional rule system is a particularly ingenious engineering design. It utilizes Glob patterns to precisely define the scope of rules, achieving an intelligent mechanism of "delivering the appropriate information into the context at the exact right moment." This design completely abandons the extensive mode of dumping all rules uniformly in front of Claude, ensuring the purity and efficiency of the context.

Regarding the writing paradigm, "less is more" is by no means a hollow slogan; there is serious technical principle support behind it. The context window is the most scarce strategic resource for the model, and every Token carries a high opportunity cost. Forcibly stuffing irrelevant information into `CLAUDE.md` is tantamount to forcibly squeezing non-passengers into a subway car during rush hour—this not only occupies precious space, but also causes the "critical information" that actually needs to board to be squeezed out, unable to enter the model's field of vision. An excellent `CLAUDE.md` should be like a refined combat memo, strictly adhering to the following 3 iron laws:

- Necessity: Every rule is information that Claude must know and cannot infer on its own.
- Operability: Every rule is a specific, clear, and executable instruction.
- Vulnerability Testing: If any rule is deleted, it will directly cause Claude to make mistakes or deviate from expectations. In Chapter 3, we will formally step into the vast world of Skills. If `CLAUDE.md` is the "onboarding manual" endowing Claude with foundational cognition, then Skills are the "professional training materials" tailor-made for it. When your "onboarding manual" becomes increasingly bloated, attempting to stuff all the complex domain knowledge into it, it will not only result in a waste of context resources but also lower the response efficiency of core rules. At this time, you will need a more elegant and intelligent on-demand loading mechanism. This is exactly the core value of the Skills system: allowing professional knowledge to be precisely injected into the dialogue context exactly at the moment it is truly needed.
