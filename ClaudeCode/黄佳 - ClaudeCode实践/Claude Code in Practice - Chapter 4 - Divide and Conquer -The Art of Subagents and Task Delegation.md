
## Divide and Conquer: The Art of Subagents and Task Delegation

### An army of a thousand is easy to find, but a good general is hard to seek.

At 3 PM on Thursday, Xiao Bing's expression looked like a stack about to overflow. She stared intently at the conversation log with Claude on the screen—the dialogue had lasted for over an hour, and the context was completely stuffed with log snippets, test outputs, and code review details. To troubleshoot an online bug in the payment module, she first had Claude analyze over 500 lines of error logs; then she ran a round of regression testing, pouring over 200 lines of test output into the chatbox; immediately after, she asked Claude to read 7 related source files. At this moment, when she asked Claude to provide a fix based on the previous analysis, Claude's response started to become bizarre; not only did it "forget" the key error code discovered in the log analysis, but it also misjudged an irrelevant failure in the test output as the core issue, and even referenced a completely non-existent function name in its suggested fix. "It's gone mad," Xiao Bing muttered to herself. She tried to redescribe the problem, but Claude's answers continued to waver among the log fragments, test noise, and code details, much like a person surrounded by a cacophony of sound waves, no longer able to hear any single voice clearly. Xiao Xue leaned in, glanced at the screen, and instantly saw through the crux of the problem, reminding her: "You stuffed all the information into the same dialogue window. The logs, test results, and code review contents combined probably amount to tens of thousands of Tokens. Claude hasn't 'forgotten,' it has been overwhelmed by the flood of information."

#### 4.1 The Dilemma of the Context Window

Xiao Bing and Xiao Xue took this problem to Brother Ka. After listening to their description, Brother Ka was not in a rush to give an answer; instead, he turned around and drew an office desk on the whiteboard. "Imagine you have an office desk in front of you," Brother Ka explained, "The size of the desktop is fixed, and it can only hold about 20 documents. Now, you need to handle 3 tasks simultaneously: review a 20-page financial report, analyze a stack of system logs, and write a technical proposal. If you pile all the documents involved in these 3 tasks on this desk, what will happen?" "The desk will be a mess, and you won't be able to find what you need at all," Xiao Bing replied. "It's not just about not finding things." Brother Ka casually drew another diagram on the whiteboard illustrating a severely imbalanced signal-to-noise ratio in the context window (see Figure 4-1), "When you start writing the technical proposal, your eyes sweep across the desk, and all you see are financial figures and log stacks. These irrelevant pieces of information are not static; they will continuously interfere with your thinking, tempting you to involuntarily bring the wording style of the financial report into the technical proposal. This is exactly the predicament you just encountered—context pollution." ![[Pasted image 20260701100249.png]] _(Figure 4-1 Severely imbalanced signal-to-noise ratio in the context window)_ Although Claude's context window is massive—about 200,000 Tokens, equivalent to a medium-thick book—this does not mean it is infinite, nor does it mean "big" equals "easy to use." Hidden here is a key issue overlooked by many developers—**Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR)**. When you stuff 500 lines of error logs into the context window, the truly valuable information might only be 3 lines of the error stack; when you pour in 200 lines of test output, **what you truly need might just be the summary sentence: "3 tests failed, 47 passed." The remaining hundreds of lines are all noise; they not only occupy precious context space but also dilute the truly important information**, causing Claude to struggle to focus on the core issue. This problem is not unfamiliar in software engineering.

- Why do operating systems need process isolation? Because if all programs share the same memory space, a wild pointer from one program could crash the entire system.
- Why did microservices architecture replace monolithic applications? Because if all business logic is crammed into a single process, a memory leak in any one module would drag down the whole thing. Separation of concerns, process isolation, bulkhead pattern—these classic software engineering principles essentially answer the same question: **How to prevent different tasks from interfering with each other**. The "context pollution" Claude encounters is exactly the "memory leak" and "process interference" of the large model era. "So what should we do? Start a new conversation every time we do a new task?" Xiao Bing asked somewhat eagerly. "That's too crude," Brother Ka shook his head. "Starting a new conversation directly means you will completely lose all context, including those truly valuable background information and project specifications. It's like throwing the entire desk along with the documents on it into a shredder just to clear the desk." He paused, his gaze sweeping across their faces, and gave the critical answer: "The correct approach is to delegate the task to SubAgents."

#### 4.2 The Essence of SubAgents

Brother Ka turned around and drew a new picture on the whiteboard. This time it was no longer a crowded office desk, but a modern office with a clear layout. "Let's think about this problem from another angle," Brother Ka said pointing at the new drawing. "Now, you are no longer a hard-working clerk, but the CEO of a company. Laid out in front of you are 3 urgent matters: auditing financial statements, troubleshooting system failures, and writing the quarterly strategic plan. Would you choose to lock yourself in a room and try to complete all 3 of these things by yourself?" "Of course not," Xiao Xue answered without hesitation, "I would let the CFO audit the financial statements and assign an SRE (Site Reliability Engineering) to troubleshoot the system failure. I just need to sit in the office, wait for them to submit their refined reports, and then make the final decision based on those reports." "Exactly correct!" A hint of approval flashed in Brother Ka's eyes. "This is the core idea of SubAgents. You don't need to have Claude swallow all the raw data; instead, let it play the role of the CEO, breaking down specific tasks and delegating them to specialized 'employees'." Immediately, he wrote down 3 keywords in the center of the whiteboard and drew the hierarchical relationship between them (see Figure 4-2). ![[Pasted image 20260701100524.png]] _(Figure 4-2 CEO Delegation Model — The Core Idea of SubAgents)_ The definition of a SubAgent is concise and clear: it is a **Claude instance equipped with an independent context window, restricted tool permissions, and a clearly defined task scope**. The main Agent initiates SubAgents as needed and passes task descriptions; the SubAgent executes the task within its independent context space and ultimately returns only the conclusions to the main Agent, rather than all the details from the process. This mechanism contains three core values, corresponding to 3 principles in software engineering respectively.

##### 1 Isolation

SubAgents possess independent context windows; the file contents they read, the command execution outputs, and intermediate reasoning processes are all strictly retained within their own context spaces and never flow back to the main dialogue. This is akin to the process isolation mechanism of an operating system—each process monopolizes an address space, and a memory leak in a single process will not affect others. In this scenario, the "noise" of 500 lines of error logs is completely sealed inside the SubAgent; the main dialogue only receives the refined fault localization conclusion, fundamentally solving the signal-to-noise ratio problem.

##### 2 Constraint

Users can configure strict tool permission whitelists for each SubAgent. For example, a code review SubAgent is only granted the 3 read-only tool permissions: Read, Grep, and Glob; even if Claude misjudges the intent, it cannot modify any files. This constraint does not rely on easily ignored suggestions in the Prompt like "please do not modify files," but rather builds a physical-level boundary. Just like assigning read-only file system permissions to a user, regardless of the user's intent, the system level directly prohibits write operations. This is the manifestation of the Principle of Least Privilege in software security.

##### 3 Reuse

SubAgents are defined in Markdown format, supporting team sharing and project migration. A code review SubAgent carefully designed by developers can be used directly by new employees upon onboarding, without requiring repeated debugging and training. This feature practices the componentization philosophy in software engineering, which is to encapsulate core capabilities into reusable standardized modules.

From a technical implementation perspective, when the main Agent decides to delegate a task, Claude Code launches a brand new child process. This child process has an independent context window, which only loads the System Prompt, project configuration file (`CLAUDE.md`), SubAgent definition instructions, and the task description passed by the main Agent. In this pure context, the child process independently executes operations such as file reading, command running, and result analysis; after the task is completed, it only sends a summary of the execution results back to the main process. The main dialogue ultimately receives only a concise paragraph of conclusion text, while all intermediate interaction processes within the SubAgent are completely released with the termination of the child process. What does this mean? Let's return to Xiao Bing's scenario: if she delegated the log analysis task to a SubAgent named `log-analyzer`, those 500 lines of redundant logs would be completely digested inside the SubAgent; the main dialogue interface would only present a single refined conclusion: "Root cause localization: `src/payment/charge.ts:89` missing null check, leading to `NullPointerException`." Thereafter, when she asked Claude to generate a fix, its context was free from log noise interference, leaving only the precise fault localization conclusion as the basis for decision-making.

The YAML Frontmatter defines "what" the SubAgent is: clarifying its identity, capability boundaries (such as tool permission whitelists), and runtime configuration parameters. The Markdown body instructions specify "how" the SubAgent acts: detailing the specific execution workflows, standardized output formats, and the required professional domain knowledge. It only possesses the permission to observe code and is strictly prohibited from modifying any files; meanwhile, its review results will follow a fixed structured format for output, allowing the main Agent to efficiently extract key information. In this definition file, every field of the YAML Frontmatter carries critical design intent and is worth deep interpretation.

- `name` (unique identifier): is the "ID card" of the SubAgent. It exists as the unique key in system logs, debug traces, and internal call chains.
- `description` (trigger description / routing basis): is the "billboard" of the SubAgent, functioning similarly to a "skill manual" in traditional programming. It is the core basis for the main Agent to perform task routing.
- Semantic matching: when a user issues an instruction (e.g., "help me review this PR"), the main Agent scans the `description` fields of all registered SubAgents.
- Intent recognition: by comparing the semantic similarity between the user intent and the description text, the main Agent judges whether the current task falls within the capability scope of that SubAgent.
- Automatic delegation: once it finds that the description of the `code-reviewer` SubAgent highly matches needs like "code review" or "security audit," the main Agent will automatically package the task context and delegate it, without needing the user to explicitly specify the SubAgent's name.
- `tools` (the "tool whitelist" building the security defense line): is the most security-valuable feature in the SubAgent mechanism; it establishes the "Principle of Least Privilege" from the system's foundation. In the aforementioned definition file, the whitelist for `code-reviewer` only contains 3 read-only operations: Read (reading file content), Grep (searching for specific patterns in files), and Glob (matching file paths). Any tools not listed here (such as Edit, Write, Bash) are entirely invisible and uncallable to this SubAgent.
- `model` (specifies the model adopted by the SubAgent): is an often-underestimated cost-optimization lever: for simple and patterned tasks (like formatting checks, log filtering), lightweight, fast-responding models like Haiku can be chosen; while for tasks requiring deep understanding and complex reasoning (like architecture design, security audits), high-performance models like Sonnet or Opus should be used. Reasonably selecting the model can effectively control costs while ensuring task quality.
- `permissionMode`: used to control the permission mode. When set to `plan`, the SubAgent enters a system-level read-only safeguard mode—this is a global security mechanism even stricter than the tool whitelist; the default value `default` follows the standard permission control flow. Let's look at another example of an execution SubAgent—a test runner. The key difference from the previous example is that this SubAgent needs to call the Bash tool to execute commands, so its tool permissions are broader, but its requirements for output format are stricter. Please note, **this SubAgent selected Haiku as the model. Tasks like running tests, parsing outputs, and extracting failure information do not require top-tier reasoning capabilities; Haiku is not only competent but also more advantageous in speed and cost. This is just like not needing to hire a chief architect to execute unit tests**. After a SubAgent is defined, there are primarily two ways to use it.
- Automatic delegation: the user only needs to make a regular request to Claude, and it will automatically judge whether the work needs to be delegated to a specific SubAgent based on the nature of the task.

One is `skills`, which allows preloading specific Skill knowledge packages for the SubAgent. #### 4.4 5 SubAgent Patterns

After understanding the basic concepts and configuration methods of SubAgents, we officially enter the core of this chapter—the 5 SubAgent patterns. This is by no means a simple classification checklist, but 5 distinctly different architectural paradigms; each precisely corresponds to specific task characteristics and is deeply rooted in software engineering principles.

##### 4.4.1 Read-only Pattern: The Safe Observer

The read-only pattern is the most fundamental and safest SubAgent pattern; its core characteristic can be summarized as reading without writing. Typical application scenarios for this pattern include code reviews, security audits, architecture analysis, and dependency checks. The common feature of these tasks is: they are essentially "observational" activities that require reading massive amounts of information and conducting deep analysis, but absolutely do not bring any side effects to the system. Just as a code reviewer should point out issues rather than directly modify the code, an auditor should reveal financial loopholes rather than arbitrarily tamper with the ledger. The tool permissions of a read-only SubAgent are strictly limited to the three core tools: Read, Grep, and Glob. These three cover all the capabilities required for code analysis: Read is used for reading file content, Grep is used for searching for specific patterns, and Glob is used for finding file paths matching rules. Their common characteristic is: no matter how they are called, they will not produce any modifications to the file system. From a software security perspective, this is exactly a perfect embodiment of the Principle of Least Privilege—granting an entity the exact permissions required to fulfill its responsibilities, no more, no less. In traditional software systems, we configure read-only permissions for database read replicas, or use `chmod` in the Linux OS to precisely control file read, write, and execute permissions; the tool whitelist mechanism of SubAgents is precisely the natural extension of this classic security philosophy in the AI Agent domain. Setting `permissionMode` to the `plan` mode can provide a higher tier of security safeguard. Under this mode, not only is the SubAgent restricted to the read-only toolset, but its entire session is marked as "read-only" by the system. This is not just a restriction on tool calls, but a system-level security commitment, eliminating any potential write risks from an architectural level.

##### 4.4.2 Execution Pattern: The High-Noise Task Processor

The execution SubAgent is the most widely applied pattern in daily development; its core value can be summarized as "signal-to-noise ratio optimization": inputting massive data (e.g., 500 lines) and outputting only refined conclusions (e.g., 5 lines). Whether it's the thousands of lines of logs generated by running a test suite, tens of thousands of records involved in analyzing system records, or hundreds of file changes triggered by code formatting, developers typically only need to focus on the key results: which tests failed, what the root cause of the fault is, and what content was specifically modified. The common feature of such tasks is: the process is accompanied by a massive amount of redundant noise, while the final value is highly condensed. If this noise is allowed to pour into the main dialogue, it will lead to a sharp decline in the signal-to-noise ratio of the information; however, by processing it through an execution SubAgent within an isolated space, the noise can be effectively blocked outside the "bulkhead," ensuring the main dialogue focuses on the core conclusions. The previously defined `test-runner` is a typical execution SubAgent. Because it needs to call the Bash tool to run test commands, its permission scope is naturally broader than the read-only pattern. However, this "broadness" is strictly constrained—through the tool whitelist mechanism, the usage boundaries of Bash can be precisely defined. For example, configuring `Bash(npm test:*)` means the SubAgent is only permitted to execute commands starting with `npm test:`. This restriction fundamentally blocks the possibility of the SubAgent executing arbitrary other Shell operations, ensuring that while granting the SubAgent execution capabilities, the system's security baseline is still firmly held. The design core of the execution SubAgent lies in the strict constraint on output format. In the SubAgent's definition file, the structural boundaries and content scope of the output must be clearly specified. Instructions such as "prohibit including full test logs" and "output summary information only" ensure that the content returned to the main dialogue is a refined conclusion, rather than raw noise data. From a software engineering perspective, the execution SubAgent is precisely a typical application of the Facade Pattern. This pattern includes the following elements.

- Complex subsystem: such as the exhaustive logs generated by a test framework, or the interaction details of underlying systems.
- Simplified interface: the structured summary provided externally by the SubAgent.
- Client: the main dialogue does not need to know the complex internal operations of the subsystem; it only needs to obtain concise, usable results through this "facade" interface.

##### 4.4.3 Parallel Pattern: Multi-Expert Workflow

When you need to deeply analyze the same problem from multiple independent dimensions, the parallel SubAgent pattern shows its unique power (see Figure 4-3). This is like simultaneously dispatching 3 detectives to handle a case together, each focusing on a different dimension of clues: Detective A focuses on investigating physical evidence, Detective B is responsible for interviewing witnesses, and Detective C investigates and analyzes surveillance footage. ![[Pasted image 20260701101026.png]] _(Figure 4-3 Parallel SubAgents — Multiple Experts Working Simultaneously)_ The 3 SubAgents carry out their work synchronously in their respective isolated spaces, without interfering with each other, greatly shortening the overall analysis time. Ultimately, the system will aggregate their individual findings to form a comprehensive, multi-perspective consolidated report. The parallel pattern has a strict prerequisite: the sub-tasks must be completely independent, with no shared states or dependency relationships. Under this pattern, a security audit does not need to wait for the results of a code quality assessment; a performance analysis also does not depend on the conclusions of a security audit. These 3 SubAgents can truly run synchronously in their respective isolated spaces without interfering with each other, thereby maximizing the use of computational resources and shortening overall time consumption. However, once a logical dependency exists in the task chain (for example, "the bug must first be located before it can be fixed"), the parallel pattern is no longer applicable. If forced to run in parallel at this time, it will lead to subsequent tasks failing or producing erroneous results due to the lack of prerequisite inputs. For such task flows with clear sequential order, the pipeline pattern introduced in Section 4.4.4 should be adopted to ensure the rigor of the execution logic. The parallel pattern has a widely known counterpart in the software engineering field—MapReduce. In the application scenario of SubAgents, this classic architecture gets a perfect mapping. In the Map phase, the main Agent acts as the "dispatcher," breaking down a grand and complex problem into multiple mutually independent sub-tasks and distributing them to different SubAgents (worker nodes). Each SubAgent processes its own portion of data or logic in parallel within an isolated environment. In the Reduce phase, when all SubAgents complete their computations, the main Agent intervenes again, responsible for collecting, integrating, and refining the output conclusions of each SubAgent, ultimately generating a unified and complete answer. Under this architecture, SubAgents focus on their own independent computational logic, without needing to care about global states or the results of other nodes; whereas the main Agent focuses on orchestration, i.e., it is responsible for task decomposition and result aggregation. A major advantage of parallel SubAgents lies in specialization. Each SubAgent can carry specific domain knowledge; for example, `security-auditor` is proficient in OWASP Top 10, `quality-checker` is good at identifying code "smells," and `perf-analyzer` is familiar with common performance anti-patterns. In contrast, an Agent attempting to be "all-powerful" might only achieve a 70-point level across the aforementioned 3 dimensions; whereas 3 "specialist" SubAgents can reach high standards of 90 points in their respective fields. This architecture maps the specialized division of labor concept in software teams—just as frontend engineers, backend engineers, and database administrators each perform their own duties, their collaborative output is often superior to the quality of a solo full-stack developer. In Claude Code, you can use the shortcut `Ctrl+B` to move a SubAgent to run in the background. When multiple SubAgents run simultaneously in the background, they will achieve true parallel execution. At this time, the main dialogue thread will not be blocked; you can continue to process other tasks and review the results after the SubAgents finish execution.

##### 4.4.4 Pipeline Pattern: Serial Processing Chain

While parallel SubAgents are suitable for independent tasks, pipeline SubAgents are applicable to tasks with clear phase dependencies (see Figure 4-4). The most classic case is the bug-fixing pipeline: first, locate the root cause of the problem; next, implement the fix based on the localization results; then, verify the effectiveness of the fix; and finally, analyze the scope of impact of the fix. In this process, the output of each phase serves as the input for the next phase, and the order of execution cannot be reversed. ![[Pasted image 20260701101051.png]] _(Figure 4-4 Pipeline SubAgents — Bug Fixing Pipeline)_ The aforementioned pipeline possesses a subtle yet crucial design feature: the tool permissions for each phase are entirely different. `bug-locator` possesses only read-only permissions, ensuring nothing is modified while locating the problem; `bug-fixer` holds read/write and Bash execution permissions, so it can edit code and run build commands; `bug-verify` has read-only and Bash execution permissions, only needing to run tests to verify the fix's validity, and is strictly forbidden from making any code changes; `bug-report` holds read-only permissions, because impact analysis only requires reviewing code and change histories. This dynamic adjustment of permissions is a specific security mechanism of the pipeline pattern, ensuring that each phase only holds the minimum permissions required to fulfill its duties. The key technical detail of the pipeline pattern lies in the Handoff Contract: the output format of each phase must strictly match the input requirements of the next phase. This **rigorous formatting contract between upstream and downstream** forms the foundation for the reliable operation of the pipeline pattern. In the software engineering field, this mechanism corresponds to the **Chain of Responsibility Pattern**: requests are passed sequentially along a processing chain, and after each processor completes its own duties, the results are handed over to the next link. The pipe mechanism in Unix/Linux operating systems also reflects the same idea, such as the command `cat log.txt | grep ERROR | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn`, where each command focuses solely on a single task, and the output of the previous command acts directly as the input for the next. ##### 4.4.5 Team Pattern: Self-Organized Collaboration Mechanism

The team pattern is the most architecturally complex and most capable among the SubAgent patterns. It holds a fundamental difference from the 4 patterns introduced earlier: in the previous 4 patterns, SubAgents are active only during task execution and terminate once the task is completed, showing a "one-off" characteristic; whereas in the team pattern, SubAgents possess long-term continuity; they act like a real team, capable of sustained collaboration, real-time communication, and autonomous division of labor. An Agent Team is composed of 3 core elements (see Figure 4-5).

- Team Lead: assumes the responsibilities of task decomposition, resource scheduling, and progress tracking; its role is similar to a project manager.
- Teammates: composed of SubAgents with different professional expertise, each responsible for specific functional modules.
- Shared Infrastructure: includes two key mechanisms. The Task List ensures all members can grasp the overall progress and personal to-dos in real-time; the Mailbox supports asynchronous communication among members (for example, a frontend developer can use this mechanism to confirm API interface specifications with a backend developer). ![[Pasted image 20260701101335.png]] _(Figure 4-5 Team SubAgents — Multi-session Self-Organized Collaboration)_ Team SubAgents are applicable to 4 typical collaboration modes.
- **Competing Hypotheses Mode**: when faced with complex, inconclusive problems (for example, whether a performance bottleneck stems from database queries or network I/O), multiple SubAgents can be dispatched to conduct parallel investigations based on different hypothetical directions. Ultimately, the Team Lead compares the multiple investigation reports and selects the conclusion with the most sufficient evidence.
- **Parallel Review Mode**: for the same code change, security review and functional review can be arranged simultaneously. The two reviewers operate independently without interfering with each other, and finally, their review comments are merged. This mode is not only more efficient than serial reviews, but the independence of multiple perspectives can also significantly improve review quality.
- **Module Ownership Mode**: in large-scale refactoring tasks, the codebase is divided by modules and assigned to different team members. For example, the frontend SubAgent is responsible for UI (User Interface) component refactoring, the backend SubAgent for API layer refactoring, and the data layer SubAgent for database migration. Each SubAgent delves deeply into its assigned module and coordinates cross-module interface changes via the Mailbox.
- **Plan Approval Mode**: one SubAgent proposes a refactoring plan, while another SubAgent plays the role of "devil's advocate," specifically raising challenges and questions. The Team Lead makes the final decision based on arguments from both sides. This adversarial design can effectively avoid blind spots in the plan. When should the team pattern be adopted over simple SubAgent patterns? A practical standard of judgment lies in collaboration complexity: if **multiple rounds of communication and deep coordination are required among SubAgents**, the team pattern should be selected; if each SubAgent only needs to execute a task once to conclude, simple parallel or pipeline patterns are sufficient. It is worth noting that the operational overhead of team SubAgents is higher—long-term maintenance of the context window means continuous Token consumption. Therefore, only in scenarios that truly require complex collaboration is it cost-effective to adopt this pattern. **Brother Ka's Remarks** The selection of the 5 patterns is not mutually exclusive; they can be combined flexibly. For example, inside a certain phase of the pipeline pattern, the parallel pattern can be embedded; e.g., in the `bug-locator` phase, dispatching a "log analyzer" and a "code searcher" to work in parallel to speed up localization. However, the nesting levels should not be too deep. Nesting more than two layers will significantly increase system complexity and debugging difficulty. If you find yourself needing more than 3 layers of nesting, this is usually a signal indicating that the current task decomposition method might need redesigning. For most daily development scenarios, using only the read-only and execution patterns is typically sufficient. Remember not to over-engineer.

#### 4.5 Collaboration Between SubAgents and Skills

In the 5 SubAgent patterns introduced in Section 4.4, although SubAgents demonstrated excellent task decomposition and isolation capabilities, a core question was sidestepped: where exactly does the "professional knowledge" of a SubAgent originate? Take `bug-fixer` in the pipeline pattern as an example; when it is delegated to fix a null check bug, although equipped with a full set of tools like Read, Edit, Write, and Bash, it knows nothing about the project's coding standards—should error handling use the `Result` pattern or a `try-catch` statement? Should logging call `console.log` or a project-specific logger? Should variable naming follow `camelCase` or `snake_case`? If these specifications are written into the project's `CLAUDE.md`, the SubAgent can indeed inherit them automatically. However, `CLAUDE.md` typically only covers general standards and struggles to encompass deep professional knowledge such as a "secure coding checklist to follow when fixing bugs"—the latter clearly belongs to the realm of Skills. There are only two atomic patterns in the combination architecture of Skills and SubAgents; their core difference lies in the relationship of inclusion (see Figure 4-6). ![[Pasted image 20260701101535.png]] _(Figure 4-6 Combination Architecture of Skills and SubAgents)_

##### Direction A: SubAgent Preloads Skill (SubAgent Includes Skill)

Under this mode, the SubAgent's definition file preloads one or more Skills as a domain knowledge base during the startup phase via the `skills` field.

- Role Positioning: the SubAgent is the executor, while the Skill is the "operation manual" in its hands.
- Application Scenarios: roles in a pipeline that require specific domain knowledge. For example, `bug-fixer` needs to load secure coding specifications, and `doc-generator` needs to load the documentation generation flow.

##### Direction B: Skill Derives SubAgent (Skill Includes SubAgent)

Under this mode, by configuring `context: fork`, the Skill automatically creates an isolated SubAgent to execute the task when triggered, ensuring intermediate processes do not pollute the main dialogue context.

- Role Positioning: the Skill is the dispatcher, and the SubAgent is the execution instance.
- Application Scenarios: deep code analysis, batch document generation, and other heavy tasks requiring strict isolation and one-time completion. The core difference between these two modes lies in the ownership of the System Prompt's control.
- Direction A: the SubAgent's definition file (the `.md` body) constitutes the System Prompt, while the full content of the Skill is injected as domain knowledge context.
- Direction B: the Agent type specified by the `agent` field provides the System Prompt, while the content of `SKILL.md` is passed in as specific task instructions. Although the underlying mechanisms of the two are completely identical, their invocation relationships are diametrically opposed. Next, we will deeply analyze Direction A through two examples. First is the improved version of `bug-fixer`. The corresponding Skill definition is as follows. It should be noted that `user-invocable: false` means this Skill does not need to be displayed in the user's `/` menu; it belongs to purely "reference book" type content and will be automatically preloaded when the SubAgent starts. Let's look at another cross-domain example—an API documentation generation SubAgent, which strengthens its professional capabilities by preloading the "document generation Skill". Here, `skills: [api-generating]` hands an "operation manual" to the SubAgent. Relying on clear role positioning (API documentation expert) and the professional workflow embedded within the Skill (including specific steps, templates, and scripts), the SubAgent transforms from a general-purpose Agent that is a "jack of all trades, master of none" into a domain expert with "specialized expertise." These two examples—`bug-fixer` paired with `secure-coding`, and `api-doc-generator` paired with `api-generating`—reveal a clear principle of responsibility division: the SubAgent defines "identity and goal," while the Skill defines "method and standard." The specific division of labor is as follows. **SubAgent** (the `.md` configuration file) is responsible for the strategic level.
- Who (Identity): establishes role positioning, such as "you are a vulnerability fixing expert" or "you are an API documentation expert."
- What (Task): clarifies the core goal, such as "fix vulnerabilities" or "generate API docs."
- Where (Scope): defines work boundaries, such as "write to the `docs/api/` directory."
- Output (Delivery): prescribes the output format, such as "return a summary containing fix details" or "count the number of routes."

**Skill** (`SKILL.md` and attached files) is responsible for tactical execution.

- How (Flow): details operational steps, such as "Step 1: Run `detect-routes.py` -> Step 2: Analyze results."
- With What (Tools): specifies dependent resources, such as the script `scripts/detect-routes.py` or the template `templates/api-doc.md`.
- By What Standard (Norm): sets execution guidelines, such as "check the authentication middleware and mark it with a lock."
- Quality: clarifies acceptance criteria, such as "all routes have been archived, and Schema matches code strictly." This separation mechanism grants the system powerful reusability capabilities. The same `secure-coding` Skill can be preloaded by `bug-fixer` to guide bug fixes, or invoked by `code-reviewer` to audit code compliance; similarly, the `api-generating` Skill can not only assist a document generation SubAgent in creating new documentation, but also empower an API review SubAgent to check the standardization of existing documentation. In short, Skills are reusable knowledge modules, and SubAgents are the flexible consumers of this knowledge. Let's look at Direction B again—Skill derives a SubAgent. By setting `context: fork` in the YAML Frontmatter of `SKILL.md`, the Skill automatically creates an isolated SubAgent to execute the task when triggered. This mechanism ensures that intermediate processes will not pollute the main dialogue's context environment. After the user inputs `/codebase-health-check src/`, the system automatically derives a SubAgent in an isolated context. This SubAgent can independently scan all files, analyze problems, and generate reports, while the main dialogue only receives a refined final result, and the massive amount of file content and reasoning processes generated in between completely will not pollute the main context. Directions A and B each have their applicable scenarios, and the judgment standard is very intuitive: Who is the protagonist?

Direction A: Role-driven (SubAgent is the protagonist).

- Core Logic: The key to the task lies in the specific role identity and collaboration process.
- Scenario Example: The `bug-fixer` in a pipeline needs to continuously apply professional knowledge to fix vulnerabilities. At this point, the SubAgent is a long-standing "expert," and the Skill is merely a "wrench" in its toolbox.
- Conclusion: When there is a need to maintain role states, conduct multi-turn interactions, or perform complex collaboration, choose Direction A.

Direction B: Knowledge/Process-driven (Skill is the protagonist).

- Core Logic: The key to the task lies in executing a standard set of knowledge processes, while the executor itself is not important, or even needs to be isolated.
- Scenario Example: `codebase-health-check` is a standard set of inspection processes. At this point, the Skill defines "how to do it," and the SubAgent is merely an "execution container" temporarily generated to isolate the context.
- Conclusion: When a task is one-off, requires strict context isolation, or the focus lies on reusing standardized workflows, choose Direction B.

#### 4.6 Token Economics

However, cost optimization is merely one dimension of the value of SubAgents, and not even its core value. There are two other key dimensions of advantages that the Prompt caching mechanism cannot replace.

- Context Window Protection. Claude's context window capacity is limited. When massive noise data crowds out storage space, Claude may trigger the Context Compaction mechanism prematurely. During this process, key information can easily be lost because it is judged as "low priority." By strictly isolating noise in an independent context, SubAgents effectively reserve precious context space for the main dialogue, ensuring the integrity of core information.
- Response Quality Improvement. A pure context environment can significantly improve Claude's focusing ability. When dialogue history is flooded with irrelevant log records and test outputs, Claude's "attention mechanism" gets dispersed, causing a drop in generation quality—this is precisely the typical predicament Xiao Bing initially faced. The isolated architecture of SubAgents fundamentally resolves the "attention dilution" problem, ensuring the main model always conducts reasoning and answering based on high-quality, highly relevant information. Then, in what scenarios would introducing a SubAgent actually backfire and not be worth the cost? There is a concise and precise decision-making guideline here: examine the ratio between input and output volumes.
- High-volume scenarios (Input >> Output): When the task's input data volume far exceeds the final output, the value of SubAgents is particularly significant. For example, analyzing hundreds of lines of system logs solely to extract 5 lines of key conclusions.
- Low-volume scenarios (Input ≈ Output): When the input and output volumes are roughly equivalent, the isolation overhead of SubAgents no longer appears cost-effective. For example, modifying a single function or writing a section of code comments generates a limited amount of intermediate data during processing. In such scenarios, completing the task directly in the main dialogue is often simpler and more efficient, without needing to bear additional architectural complexity and communication costs. The decision to enable SubAgents can be summarized into the following 4 core dimensions.
- Large-Scale File Reading (Input Noise Isolation): Here the task requires reading a large number of files (typically over 5) or processing massive data sources. Using SubAgents can completely isolate the "input noise" generated during the file reading process in an independent environment, avoiding having the main dialogue context stuffed with redundant file content.
- High-Frequency Output Generation (Output Noise Filtering): The task is expected to generate a large amount of intermediate output, such as full test reports, detailed system logs, or complete build process records. The SubAgent acts as a "filter," returning only concise summaries or key error information to the main dialogue, thus preventing lengthy output data from diluting the main model's attention and wasting Tokens.
- Context Integrity Protection (Long-Term Task Planning): The current task is only one link in a complex workflow, and it is necessary to ensure the main dialogue retains sufficient context space to support subsequent multi-turn interactions or reasoning. By stripping away high-consumption sub-tasks, the SubAgent mechanism effectively protects the main dialogue's "context window" from being exhausted all at once, ensuring the continuity and information integrity of long-term tasks.
- Operating Permissions and Security Boundaries (Risk Management): The task involves sensitive operations (such as file system writes, network requests, or executing external commands) or requires strict permission control. The SubAgent can be configured as an independent sandbox environment with restricted permissions. Even if the sub-task execution fails or exhibits malicious behavior, its impact is limited to a localized scope, thereby providing a natural security firewall for the main system.

#### 4.7 Viewing SubAgents Through Software Engineering

Reading up to here, an astute reader may have already perceived: the SubAgent architecture has not invented any disruptive new concepts out of thin air. Its core essence is creatively migrating and mapping the classic wisdom accumulated over decades in the software engineering field into the architectural design of AI Agents. This migration is by no means accidental, but an inevitable logical evolution. Because an AI Agent is essentially a complex software system, it inevitably follows the general laws and core principles of software systems engineering.

##### 1 Single Responsibility Principle

The Single Responsibility Principle ranks first among the five major principles of object-oriented design. Its core definition: a class (or module) should have only one reason to change—in other words, it should focus solely on doing one thing well. The SubAgent architecture is exactly the perfect reproduction and practice of this classic principle in the AI Agent domain.

- Specialization of Duties: `code-reviewer` focuses solely on code quality reviews; `test-runner` is only responsible for executing test cases and collecting results; `bug-locator` is dedicated purely to locating the root cause of a bug. Each SubAgent is like a highly cohesive, independent class, performing its own duties without overstepping its bounds.
- Declarative Duty Manual: The Prompt file for each SubAgent (such as `code-reviewer.md`) is essentially its "duty manual."
- Change Isolation and Maintainability: For example, if there is a need to adjust the strictness of code reviews or introduce new security standards, developers only need to modify this single `code-reviewer.md` file.

##### 2 Bulkhead Pattern

The Bulkhead Pattern stems from the wisdom of shipbuilding engineering: the hull of a cargo ship is divided into multiple independent watertight compartments by sturdy bulkheads. Even if one compartment unfortunately takes on water, the seawater is restricted to that local area, never causing the entire giant ship to sink. In microservices architecture, this pattern is widely adopted to prevent single-point failures from causing cascading avalanches. And in the AI Agent architecture, the context isolation mechanism of SubAgents is precisely the perfect mapping of the Bulkhead Pattern.

- Fault Tripping and Boundary Locking: If the `log-analyzer` SubAgent encounters abnormal data (such as an infinite loop, hallucination outbreak, or formatting crash) while processing massive logs, the resulting "failure" will be strictly locked within the SubAgent's independent sandbox.
- Main System Stability Guarantee: Because of the "bulkhead's" existence, the chaos of sub-tasks will not pollute the context environment of the main dialogue.
- Graceful Degradation: This design ensures the resilience of the system.

##### 3 MapReduce Pattern

The MapReduce pattern originates from the distributed computing field and was first proposed by Google in a related paper in 2004. This pattern breaks down large-scale data processing into two phases: Map and Reduce. The Map phase is responsible for distributing tasks to multiple worker nodes for parallel processing, while the Reduce phase is responsible for summarizing the calculation results of all nodes. The parallel pattern is precisely the innovative application of the MapReduce pattern in the AI Agent domain. In this pattern, the main Agent assumes the coordinator role, responsible for distributing tasks (Map) and summarizing conclusions (Reduce), while the various SubAgents act as worker nodes, focusing on executing the parallel Map computation tasks.

##### 4 Chain of Responsibility Pattern

The Chain of Responsibility Pattern originates from the GoF design patterns; its core defines a processing chain where requests are passed along the chain until received and processed by a certain processor. The pipeline pattern is a variant of this pattern: unlike the traditional chain of responsibility where a request is terminated by "some" processor, the pipeline pattern requires "each" processor to partially process the request and pass the intermediate results to the next link. Unix pipes, Java Servlet Filter Chains, and Node.js Express middleware are all specific embodiments of this idea across different tech stacks, and the pipeline pattern is exactly the newest member of this family. These corresponding relationships are not far-fetched analogies, but rather reveal a deep underlying law: excellent architectural principles possess cross-domain universality. Whether designing microservice systems, distributed computing frameworks, or building AI Agent architectures, the fundamental challenges developers face remain consistent—how to decompose complexity, how to isolate failures, and how to achieve efficient collaboration. Engineers well-versed in classic design patterns possess a natural intuitive advantage when venturing into AI Agent design, because they have repeatedly validated the effectiveness of these ideas in other domains. After hearing this, Xiao Xue nodded thoughtfully and said: "So learning design patterns isn't just to deal with interviews; these ideas have actually become more useful in the AI era." Brother Ka nodded and said: "The core wisdom of software engineering has never become outdated; they have merely found brand new forms of expression in every new wave of technology."

#### 4.8 Practical Considerations

When actually using SubAgents, a few easily overlooked details are worth understanding in advance, as they often determine whether SubAgents can work stably in production environments. First is the inheritance relationship of `CLAUDE.md`. The `CLAUDE.md` under the project root directory is effective for all SubAgents—when a SubAgent starts, it will automatically load this "project memory." This means the coding standards, tech stack conventions, and command aliases you define in it will all be followed by the SubAgent. However, the SubAgent's own definition file can supplement or even override the rules in `CLAUDE.md`. Its order of priority is: SubAgent Definition File > Project-Level `CLAUDE.md` > User-Level `CLAUDE.md`. Second is the "message transmission" mode of the context. SubAgents cannot communicate directly with each other: each SubAgent can only obtain the content explicitly passed by the main Agent; it neither has the right to access the main dialogue's history, nor can it perceive the existence of other SubAgents. This information interaction mechanism belongs to "message transmission" rather than "shared memory"—the main Agent must extract the conclusion of SubAgent A and embed it into the task description of SubAgent B for SubAgent B to obtain the relevant information. Deeply understanding this mechanism is crucial for designing an efficient pipeline: you must ensure the output format of each phase possesses highly structured characteristics so that the main Agent can accurately extract and smoothly forward it. Then there is the interrupt recovery mechanism. If a SubAgent gets interrupted during execution due to network fluctuations or manual termination, its intermediate work results in memory will be lost. For time-consuming or critical tasks, an effective strategy is to have the SubAgent persist its intermediate products to files. For example, a log analysis SubAgent can record the analyzed range intervals and discovered anomalies in real-time into `.claude-work/log-analysis.md`. When the task needs to restart, the new SubAgent can read this file first to precisely locate the breakpoint and continue the work, thereby avoiding the repetitive labor of starting from scratch. Finally, there is the control of nesting depth. Although SubAgents support calling each other, it is recommended to keep the nesting level within two layers. Adding each layer of nesting will not only intensify the loss during the information transmission process, but also make debugging difficulty rise exponentially. If you find the business logic requires more than 3 layers of nesting, this usually means there is a flaw in the granularity of task decomposition—at this time, you should redesign the architecture, "flatten" the deep nesting, and turn to use more efficient parallel processing or pipeline structures instead.

#### Chapter Summary

SubAgents are the most architecturally significant feature in Claude Code's extension system. If `CLAUDE.md` gives Claude memory capabilities, and Skills give it professional knowledge, then what SubAgents give it is an even more fundamental capability—the ability to decompose complexity. When faced with a complex task that involves multiple dimensions, is accompanied by massive noise, and requires multi-stage processing, SubAgents provide a structured coping strategy: decomposing the overall task into multiple sub-tasks with clear focuses, executing them independently in mutually isolated contexts, and ultimately aggregating the refined core conclusions back to the main dialogue. The value of this strategy is not only reflected at the technical level (such as context isolation, permission constraints, and the optimization of Token utilization efficiency), but more deeply in the way of thinking. Learning to use SubAgents is essentially mastering a kind of "delegation thinking": no longer personally stuffing all details into a single dialogue, but acting like a mature manager, clearly defining the responsibility boundaries, capability requirements, and delivery standards for each task, and delegating it to the appropriate executor. This way of thinking inherits the exact same lineage as modular design, microservices architecture, and the separation of concerns principle in software engineering—they collectively respond to an eternal challenge: how to effectively master complexity. The 5 SubAgent patterns (read-only, execution, parallel, pipeline, and team) are not isolated techniques from one another, but a progressively advancing spectrum of capabilities. From the most basic read-only observation to the highest-level complex multi-session self-organized collaboration, they comprehensively cover various scenarios from daily development tasks to large engineering projects. Once you master these 5 patterns and the software engineering principles behind them, you will possess the intuition and capability to construct an adaptable SubAgent architecture under any new scenario. If Skills and SubAgents act mainly on Claude's "cognitive level," then Hooks extend its control to the "system execution level": it is no longer limited to instructing Claude "how to think," but directly intercepts and constrains its behavior at the physical execution layer.
